**India does not have a dedicated crypto law. Instead, it regulates crypto activity through two existing levers — taxation and anti-money-laundering rules — while a formal licensing framework, especially for derivatives, still does not exist.** For an operator eyeing one of the world's largest crypto user bases, understanding that gap is the whole game. Here's how India's approach actually works.

## Regulation by tax, not by license

India's most concrete crypto rules are tax rules. Since the Finance Act 2022, income from transferring a Virtual Digital Asset (VDA) is taxed at a flat **30%**, with no deduction for expenses and no offsetting of losses — plus a **1% tax deducted at source (TDS)** on transfers above a threshold ([ClearTax](https://cleartax.in/s/cryptocurrency-taxation-guide)). This is a taxing regime, not a licensing one: it tells you how crypto gains are taxed, not who may legally operate an exchange.

## Regulation by anti-money-laundering

The second lever is AML. In March 2023, India brought VDA-related activities under the **Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA)**, making crypto exchanges and intermediaries "reporting entities" that must run [KYC/AML](/blog/kyc-aml-for-crypto-exchanges) programs, maintain records, and report to the Financial Intelligence Unit ([FIU-IND](https://fiuindia.gov.in/)). Offshore platforms serving Indian users have been required to register with FIU-IND as well — the compliance net extends beyond domestic firms.

## The derivatives gap

Here's the crux for a trading venue: India has **no regulatory framework that permits crypto derivatives** for retail residents, and domestic exchanges do not offer them ([Global Legal Insights](https://www.globallegalinsights.com/practice-areas/blockchain-cryptocurrency-laws-and-regulations/india/)). Regulatory authority is itself unsettled — split informally across the central bank, the securities regulator, and the finance ministry, none of which has issued a crypto-derivatives licensing regime. Indians who trade crypto derivatives largely do so on offshore platforms, in a grey zone rather than a regulated market.

<Callout title="What this means for an operator">
India is a massive market, but crypto derivatives sit in a regulatory vacuum: heavily taxed, subject to AML law, and not licensable domestically. An operator can't simply "get an India license" for a derivatives venue — there isn't one. Any India strategy has to be built around that reality and qualified local legal advice, not around a framework that doesn't yet exist.
</Callout>

## India in the global picture

India is a sharp contrast to jurisdictions that built explicit regimes — compare it against the frameworks in the [Global Licensing Guide](/blog/crypto-derivatives-licensing-global-guide), or the [offshore routes](/blog/offshore-crypto-derivatives-licensing) many operators use to serve grey-zone demand. It's also a case study in the broader dynamics of [crypto in emerging markets](/blog/crypto-derivatives-emerging-markets), where demand often runs well ahead of regulation.

## The takeaway

India regulates crypto by taxing it (30% plus a 1% TDS) and by folding it into anti-money-laundering law (PMLA and FIU-IND registration) — but it has not built a licensing regime, and crypto derivatives remain outside any domestic framework. For operators, India is high-demand and high-uncertainty: approach it as a legal grey zone, not a market you can straightforwardly license into.
